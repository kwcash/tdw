# The Borrowed Knife

*How an Open System Financed Its Rival and Published Its Own Inventions to Be Copied*

A case study that turns the full apparatus built so far — every dimension, every domain, every stratagem — on a single documented maneuver. The maneuver ran for a quarter-century in plain sight, inside the deepest capital market and the most generous patent system the world has ever built.

---

## Prologue: A Hundred Shares of Nothing

On the morning of September 19, 2014, a retail investor in Granite City or Sacramento, anywhere along the American middle, could buy a hundred shares of Alibaba. It was the largest initial public offering in the history of the world to that point. It raised $21.8 billion. Six of Wall Street's largest banks stood on the cover. The New York Stock Exchange rang its bell for a Chinese company that had become a household word.

He did not buy a hundred shares of Alibaba.

He bought a hundred shares of a Cayman Islands holding company that owned no part of Alibaba's Chinese operating business and never had. What that holding company owned was a stack of contracts. Service agreements, powers of attorney, equity-pledge arrangements. They promised to route the economics of the Chinese company offshore without ever transferring ownership of it. For accounting purposes the shell could consolidate the operating company onto its balance sheet, so the statements read as though the investor owned a piece of Alibaba. He did not. He owned a claim on a claim, enforceable, if at all, only in the courts of a one-party state that had never once demonstrated it would enforce such a claim.

This was not a secret, exactly. The prospectus disclosed it, somewhere around page two hundred, in the conditional grammar of securities lawyers, beneath a front section that called the Chinese business "our" business throughout.

Seven years later the Chairman of the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission put the buried fact on the public record. Gary Gensler stated plainly, on July 30, 2021, that neither the shell company nor the investors holding its stock own any equity in the China-based operating business, and he said he worried that ordinary investors did not grasp that they held shares in a shell rather than in the real company.[^1] It is the regulator's own dated confession that for two decades no one had plainly told the people whose money was at stake the central fact of the structure.

By March 2025, when the U.S.-China Economic and Security Review Commission published its periodic tabulation, 286 Chinese companies traded on the three largest U.S. exchanges with a combined market capitalization of $1.1 trillion. Of those, the Commission counted 159 that ran on this contractual shell, the variable interest entity, or VIE. Those issuers carried roughly $1 trillion, ninety-one percent of all the Chinese market value listed in the United States.[^2] A row-by-row reading of the Commission's own table finds 161 shaded entries. The two-company difference falls among micro-caps and changes no dollar figure or conclusion.

Ninety-one percent of a trillion dollars of American and global savings, then, rode on a structure whose defining feature is that the saver owns nothing he thinks he owns.

This chapter shows how that came to pass, who built it, who profited, and what it financed on the far end. It also shows what ought to be done, with the criminal held apart from the lawful-but-harmful, and both held apart from the plain failure of policy.

A second channel, older and quieter, ran in parallel through the patent system. The same open architecture laid American inventions open to copying at the moment they were most vulnerable. Read together, the two channels are not two stories. They are one stratagem worked in two markets.

Strip away the euphemism and the shape is plain. American savers and American inventors supplied the capital and the blueprints. A handful of intermediaries collected their fee at the door. The Chinese party-state took the rest — the money, the technology, and the command over both. Beijing did not have to steal the knife. We left it on the table, and we paid the men who passed it across.

## 1. Why This Is the Case Study

The dimensions chapters set out the axes along which national contests are now waged. Capital, information, law, technology, and time. The domains chapters mapped the arenas in which those dimensions harden into concrete advantage or loss. Public markets, the audit and accounting apparatus, the patent and standards system, the retirement-savings complex, the defense-industrial base. This case asks whether the framework explains a real and complete episode, using all the dimensions and all the domains at once, and reading the result through the stratagems catalogued earlier.

The U.S. listing of Chinese companies through VIE structures, set beside the systematic mining of published U.S. patent applications, is the cleanest available test case. It earns that role for four reasons.

**First, primary sources document it to the bone.** Every load-bearing fact in this chapter rests on a primary source in the public record. SEC filings retrievable by accession number on EDGAR, dated statements and press releases on sec.gov, the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office's own dashboards and announcements, a congressional commission's tabulation, and a 2005 working paper prepared for that same commission. Almost nothing here depends on inference where a document exists.

**Second, it spans the full time axis the framework demands.** The patent channel opens with a change in U.S. law in 1999 and 2000. The capital channel runs from the first Chinese internet listings of 2000, through the Alibaba peak of 2014, the regulatory rupture of 2021, the audit confrontation of 2022, and the small-cap revival of 2024 and 2025. We can watch one logic operate across a quarter-century and measure how the instruments changed.

**Third, it crosses cleanly from the economic register into the national-security register.** It moves from a question of where wealth went into a question of whom the money funds and what it funds them to build. That crossing is precisely what the framework was built to trace.

**Fourth, and most important for the integrity of this report, responsibility here divides into real and separate categories.** Some of the conduct was criminal, and courts have punished it. Some was lawful and remains so. Some sits in the open ground between, and warrants investigation that has not yet occurred. A case study that flattens those distinctions is propaganda. A case study that holds them is evidence. This chapter holds them, and the holding is itself a demonstration of the method.

A word on the title. The classical strategic canon names a maneuver, *jiè dāo shā rén*, kill with a borrowed knife, in which one reaches one's aim not with one's own resource but with an adversary's, turning the strength of the other into the instrument of one's own advance. Whether by deliberate design in Beijing or by the emergent opportunism of thousands of separate actors — a question this chapter takes seriously rather than assuming — the pattern fits the name. The knife was the American capital market and the American patent office. It was, in the most literal sense, borrowed.

## 2. The Mechanism, Domain by Domain

### 2.1 The Listing Domain: A Structure Built to Consolidate Without Owning

A Chinese company in a sector Beijing deems sensitive cannot take foreign equity, and it cannot list its operating company abroad. The internet, data, media, education, and finance are all such sectors.

The VIE is the way around the wall. The founders register an offshore holding company, almost always in the Cayman Islands. That holding company lists its shares in New York and takes the public's cash. It then signs a sheaf of contracts with the onshore operating company, the entity that holds the licenses, employs the people, and earns the revenue. The contracts sweep the operating company's profits up to the shell and hand the shell's directors the practical levers of control. No equity ever crosses the border. Under U.S. accounting rules the shell consolidates the operating company as a variable interest entity, so the statements presented to American investors describe a business the investors do not own.

The strategic canon has a name for this too. *Mán tiān guò hǎi*, cross the sea by deceiving the heavens. It means moving in the open by making the extraordinary look routine. The VIE is not hidden in a vault. It is hidden in the footnotes, normalized by repetition, made invisible by the very disclosures meant to reveal it. For two decades the structure crossed the sea every day. The heavens — the investing public and, for a long time, their regulator — looked at the consolidated income statement and saw a company.

The scale, again, from the Commission's March 2025 count. 159 VIE issuers, roughly $1 trillion, ninety-one percent of all U.S.-listed Chinese value. The roster is no fringe of obscure names. It includes Alibaba, Pinduoduo, NetEase, JD.com, Trip.com, Baidu, Li Auto, KE Holdings, Tencent Music, Bilibili, NIO, iQIYI, and Weibo. These are the commanding heights of China's consumer internet, its electric-vehicle sector, its data-cloud infrastructure, its artificial-intelligence champions. The Commission's report carries the full enumeration, with market capitalizations, IPO dates and proceeds, sectors, and the underwriting syndicate for each.

The disclosed primary proceeds of these offerings, summed across the cohort's history, run into the tens of billions of dollars. Alibaba's 2014 offering alone raised $21.8 billion. The headline IPO figure understates the true flow by a wide margin, because it omits follow-on offerings, convertible issuance, and the far larger volume of secondary-market purchases through which American institutions and index funds bought shares from earlier holders, often Chinese and Sino-foreign venture funds, and cashed them out at U.S. valuations. Primary issuance is the front door. Most of the money walked in through the secondary market.

### 2.2 The Audit Domain: The Decade the Books Could Not Be Checked

A security is only as trustworthy as the audit behind it. For most of this period, no one could inspect the audits behind these securities.

Congress itself built American audit oversight on the ashes of a fraud. When Enron collapsed in 2001 and dragged its auditor, Arthur Andersen, down with it, Congress answered. It passed the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 and created the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board, the PCAOB, with authority to inspect the auditors of every company that lists in the United States. Every company. The premise of the post-Enron settlement was simple. No issuer's books would go unexamined.

China was the exception for two decades. The China and Hong Kong affiliates of the major accounting firms performed the audits of U.S.-listed Chinese companies, and the Chinese government, invoking state-secrecy and sovereignty grounds, refused to let the PCAOB inspect them. A 2013 memorandum of understanding produced a paper mechanism and almost no access. Over the next nine years, by the PCAOB's own account, Chinese authorities blocked the inspections that American law required of every other issuer on the exchange. The body Congress created to verify them could not verify the financial statements that supported a trillion dollars of valuation.

The exception held until 2022. Under the pressure of a delisting statute discussed below, the PCAOB secured an inspection agreement and, in late 2022, conducted its first inspections of mainland and Hong Kong audit firms in over a decade.[^3] The first round, announced in May 2023, was not reassuring. The board found deficiencies in seven of the eight audits it examined at the local affiliates of KPMG and PricewaterhouseCoopers. The disciplinary actions that followed are the part of this story where the system worked. Section 6 catalogues them precisely, because they bear on the question of who pays.

The decade of unverifiable books is the precondition for everything else. It is what made the next domain possible.

### 2.3 The Fraud Domain: Luckin Coffee, Documented End to End

The risk of unverifiable audits is not theoretical. The proof sits, step by step, in a single company's own SEC submissions.

Luckin Coffee raised $561 million in a 2019 Nasdaq IPO, marketed as the technology-driven challenger to Starbucks in China. At its peak it carried a market capitalization near $12 billion. On January 31, 2020, an anonymous short-seller's report alleged that Luckin had fabricated the numbers. On February 3, 2020, in a 6-K now on EDGAR, Luckin "categorically denies all allegations," calling the report's methodology flawed and its evidence unsubstantiated.

The denial was false. By July 1, 2020, the company's own Special Committee, having reviewed more than 550,000 documents and interviewed more than sixty witnesses, reported that the fabrication had begun in April 2019 and had inflated 2019 net revenue by roughly RMB 2.12 billion. Nasdaq had already delisted the stock on July 13, 2020.[^4]

Two penalties followed, and the gap between them is the lesson. In September 2020, China's State Administration for Market Regulation fined the Luckin entities and their accomplice companies a combined RMB 61 million, roughly nine million dollars, for fabricating more than $300 million in sales. In December 2020, the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission announced a $180 million penalty in Press Release 2020-319.[^5] The SEC alleged that Luckin had fabricated more than $300 million in retail sales through three related-party schemes, inflated its expenses by more than $190 million, and built a fake operations database to hide the fraud.

The American penalty ran twenty times the Chinese one. And the SEC's own release records the final indignity. Any payment to defrauded shareholders from the penalty would require the approval of Chinese authorities. Even the restitution ran through Beijing's consent.

Luckin trades today on U.S. over-the-counter markets at a valuation the Commission puts at $8.7 billion, higher than at the IPO where it defrauded its investors. On every margin the record can measure, the deterrent was weak.

### 2.4 The Savings Domain: Whose Money This Is

The thousand billion dollars did not come, in the main, from speculators who understood the structure and priced its risk. A large share came from the deferred wages of American workers, allocated by people acting as fiduciaries. A larger share came through a channel in which no human being made a decision at all.

Take the deliberate channel first. A 2023 review by a bipartisan nonprofit found that seventy-two of the largest U.S. public pension funds had committed a combined $68 billion to China between 2020 and 2023.[^6] The California State Teachers' Retirement System reported $5.6 billion of China exposure. The New York State Teachers' fund reported $3.1 billion. CalPERS, the largest American public pension, held over $3 billion in Chinese companies, including names that had landed on U.S. security blacklists.

The federal Thrift Savings Plan, the retirement account of American servicemembers and civil servants, came within months in 2020 of shifting its international fund to a benchmark that included Chinese listings. Officials halted the move and redesigned the benchmark to exclude China and Hong Kong outright.[^7] That redesign is an admission in its own right. The United States government decided this exposure was not fit for the people who defend it.

The involuntary channel is larger and quieter. Once the index providers MSCI and FTSE Russell added Chinese American Depositary Receipts, and later mainland A-shares, to their global and emerging-market benchmarks, every American who held a target-date fund, an emerging-markets index fund, or a default 401(k) allocation became a creditor of these VIE shells automatically, by the mechanical operation of the index. A schoolteacher in Illinois did not choose to hold contractual claims on Pinduoduo's Cayman holding company. The index chose for her, and the intermediaries who built and licensed the index collected a fee for the choice.

Here the populist charge lands and the harsher word does not. The architecture made ordinary people carry a risk they neither understood nor approved. That much is true. But no one stole anything from the schoolteacher in the legal sense. She received a security in exchange for her money. The architecture took her informed consent instead — not through theft, but through a disclosure buried in footnotes, an audit no one could verify, and an allocation that ran automatically. The precision matters, and it is what makes the rest of the indictment stick.

### 2.5 The Technology Domain: The Patent System as the Older Channel

Here the case study reaches back. Pat Choate prepared the document the framework most needs for this very commission two decades ago, his 2005 working paper, *A Great Wall of Patents*.

When the United States implemented the World Trade Organization's intellectual-property agreement, it adopted a rule that orders the Patent and Trademark Office to publish the full contents of a patent application on the internet eighteen months after filing. The office must publish whether or not a patent has issued, and whether or not one ever will. This broke with two centuries of American practice, under which the office held an inventor's secrets in confidence until it granted or denied the patent. The premise of the new rule, as with the audit regime and the disclosure regime, was reciprocity. It assumed an open world in which all the major systems publish, and all enforce.

The reciprocity did not exist on the other side. Choate's paper recorded the consequence in a single devastating anecdote, drawn from a Japanese newspaper's investigation. The head of the intellectual-property unit at China's largest consumer-electronics maker described to a visiting Japanese official how the company ran dozens of computers to mine published patent applications from Japan, the United States, and Europe for material useful to its own product development — and said the practice meant the firm spent, in his words, only "a small amount of money on research."[^8] When Japanese officials began watching their own patent database, they logged seventeen thousand inquiries a day from China and fifty-five thousand from Korea. The published applications of the West's inventors had become, in Choate's phrase, a royalty-free library for the world's copyists.

The maneuver Choate named, the Great Wall of Patents, went a step past reading. Chinese entities would take a published foreign application, modify it slightly, and file it as their own in China's patent office, which, like every patent office in the world except the United States at that time, awarded the patent to the first to file rather than the first to invent. Armed with a Chinese patent on a foreigner's invention, the copyist could then sue the original inventor in Chinese courts whose appeals were slow enough to let an infringer operate for years.

Choate's recommendations, and what became of them, are the over-time test of the technology domain, and the United States failed it on every count.

He urged Congress to repeal the eighteen-month publication rule. Congress never repealed it. It remains in force in 2026, and more than 400,000 U.S. applications filed in 2024 alone feed the exposed pool.

He warned against any move from first-to-invent to first-to-file, because it would let a copyist win the race to the patent office with a stolen application. Congress enacted exactly that change in the America Invents Act of 2011, and the United States has been first-to-file since March 2013 — harmonized, as he feared, down to the rule that most rewards the fast copyist over the true originator.

He reported a 2004 examination backlog above 500,000 applications and a pendency of 27.6 months, and argued the cheapest fix was to hire examiners. By January 2025 the office's backlog of unexamined applications had reached an all-time high of 837,928, roughly sixty percent worse than the crisis he described, with total pending applications across all stages above 1.2 million and first-action pendency back around twenty months.[^9]

The window between publication and grant, during which an American invention sits exposed on the internet without protection, is as wide today as it was when Choate called it a hemorrhage.

China's own patent office, over the same period, became the largest in the world by filing volume. That is consistent with the defensive-thicket dynamic Choate described, though it does not by itself prove it. In the technology domain as in the capital domain, the United States ran an open system on the assumption of reciprocity, the reciprocity never came, and the asymmetry ran one way.

### 2.6 The Security Domain: What the Money Funds

The last domain is where the economic question becomes a national one. The capital that flowed through the VIE channel did not finance Chinese consumer staples or utilities. By the Commission's classification, more than half of the VIE cohort's trillion dollars sits in companies tagged Technology, and the rest of the top of the table is e-commerce, fintech, electric vehicles, and logistics. These are the sectors China's own industrial policy names as the high ground of national competition.

Three of the Commission's own examples carry the point.

Major Wall Street banks led Weibo's U.S. IPO. The company operates under direct government direction to censor its platform, and central and local authorities use it to surveil and suppress public protest.

The U.S. Department of Defense has identified Hesai Group's laser-sensing technology as having extensive military applications, and in 2024 the Department designated the LiDAR maker a Chinese military company on its 1260H list.[^10] That designation, the Commission notes, places no restriction whatsoever on continued American investment until a defense-procurement bar begins in 2026.

The 2024 listing wave brought the autonomous-driving companies Pony AI and WeRide to U.S. markets, raising hundreds of millions of dollars for a technology whose dual-use character no one disputes.

Beneath the specific cases lies a legal fact that hangs over every name on the list. China's 2017 National Intelligence Law provides that "any organization or citizen shall support, assist, and cooperate with state intelligence work." The 2017 Cybersecurity Law requires companies to provide technical support and assistance to public-security organs. A Beijing political analyst, quoted in the Commission's report, summarized the Communist Party's 2020 opinion on the private economy as a reminder to the firms that they remain, in his phrase, "always affiliates of the Party."[^11]

Under Chinese law, no purely private U.S.-listed Chinese company exists beyond the reach of the party-state. Every dollar of American retirement savings in these names strengthens the balance sheet of an enterprise that is, by the law of its own country, an instrument of that state on demand.

## 3. The Dimensions, Read Across the Domains

Having walked the domains, we can read the dimensions straight through them. That is the synthesis the framework requires.

**Capital.** The dimension worked as a one-way valve. Real, immediate, fungible dollars flowed toward Chinese champions. What flowed back to the American saver was a contractual claim of untested enforceability. The valve had a second feature, examined in Section 5. It minted equity for insiders. A U.S. listing did more than raise money. It created a liquid, dollar-denominated currency in which the company could pay, and enrich, its own founders, managers, and engineers.

**Information.** The asymmetry here is the sharpest, and it runs in opposite directions in the two channels. In securities, the United States demanded full and audited disclosure from its own issuers while accepting, for two decades, unverifiable disclosure from China-based issuers whose auditors it could not inspect. In patents, it demanded full publication of its own inventors' applications while China's enforcement system gave foreign holders no effective recourse. In both, an information regime designed to be symmetric ran only one way. The United States revealed. China read.

**Law.** The dimension came down to one decisive fact. Enforceability is territorial, and the territory was Beijing's. Chinese law and Chinese courts govern the VIE contract in practice, and there no court has ever shown it enforceable. The patent stolen through the Great Wall technique faces litigation in Chinese courts whose delays favor the infringer. Even the restitution to Luckin's defrauded American investors required Chinese approval. American law reached the disclosure, eventually, and the U.S.-based conduct — the Luckin fraud charges and the audit-firm sanctions. It could not reach the underlying asset, because the asset sat under a sovereign that did not recognize the American claim on it.

**Technology.** The capital channel and the patent channel converge here. American savings funded the Chinese technology base. The American patent system published the inventions that base set out to copy. The same strategic sectors — AI, autonomy, sensing, data — stand at both ends. This is where the case study's two halves become one.

**Time.** The maneuver unfolded across a quarter-century, and its instruments evolved in plain sight. The patent exposure opens the period, from 1999 to 2013. The blue-chip VIE listings build the middle, from 2000 to 2014. The regulatory rupture and audit confrontation define the inflection, in 2021 and 2022. The small-cap revival, the 2024 and 2025 wave that runs through a thinner tier of intermediaries, marks the late phase. We can date each stage, and the dating is itself evidence. This was not one event. It was a sustained process that adapted as the ground shifted.

## 4. The Stratagems

The framework's most demanding claim is that contests like this one are not random. They follow identifiable stratagems. This section names them and tests each against the record.

It also treats the question of agency honestly. To say a pattern fits a stratagem is not to prove that a single mind in Beijing ran it from a playbook. Chinese state policy directed some of what follows, beyond doubt. Some was the emergent result of thousands of separate actors — founders, banks, funds, index committees — each chasing a local advantage. The framework's value is that it describes the resulting shape. The report's integrity requires distinguishing design from emergence wherever the evidence allows.

**The borrowed knife**, funding the champion with the rival's capital, is the master maneuver of the whole episode, and the evidence for partial design is strong. China prohibits the foreign ownership that would let these companies list directly. It then permits, and since March 2023 formally approves through its securities regulator, the VIE workaround that lets them list anyway, capturing foreign capital while conceding no ownership.[^12] The state engineered the precondition. Whether it foresaw the full scale of the transfer is unknowable. That it built the structure that produced the transfer is documented.

**Crossing the sea in plain sight**, the VIE itself. Examined in Section 2.1. The extraordinary made routine, the structure hidden in the footnotes and normalized by repetition, until the regulator himself conceded that ordinary investors did not understand what they held.

**Sacrifice the plum to save the peach**, the strategic withdrawal of the state-owned enterprises. This is the clearest instance of deliberate state direction in the entire record. When the audit confrontation forced the issue, every Chinese state-owned enterprise delisted from U.S. exchanges. Eight issuers exited in 2022 and 2023, including the two big airlines with a combined market capitalization above $32 billion, and before them six energy and resource giants worth over $341 billion. They cited administrative burden. Analysts and the Commission read the timing as a decision to pull the entities whose audit papers held genuine state secrets out of American reach, while leaving the non-SOE channel — the consumer internet and the tech champions — open to keep taking American capital. Sacrifice the plum, the SOEs whose secrets no one could expose, to save the peach, the channel itself. The withdrawal was orderly, simultaneous, and policy-timed. This was not emergence.

**Watch the fire from across the river**, the regulatory rupture of 2021. When Beijing cracked down on DiDi days after its $4.4 billion New York IPO, and gutted the education sector by decree, U.S. investors absorbed catastrophic losses while the Chinese state advanced its domestic control.[^13] DiDi delisted within a year. The education names lost the better part of their value overnight. The losses fell on the foreign holder. The strategic gain accrued to the Party. Whether the investor harm was intended or merely tolerated, the structure ensured that the fire burned on the American bank of the river.

**Loot a burning house**, the patent channel. The eighteen-month publication rule built a burning house. Tens of thousands of valuable American applications stood exposed every year in the gap between publication and grant. The copyists, as Choate documented, walked in and took what they wanted, royalty-free, sometimes patenting it back in China to sue the inventor. This was emergent far more than directed — a million separate acts of opportunism enabled by one structural vulnerability the United States wrote into its own law and never repaired.

The honest synthesis across the stratagems is this. The SOE withdrawal and the construction of the VIE channel show real state design. The patent mining and much of the secondary-market capital flow are emergent exploitation of structural openings. The framework does not need a single conspiratorial hand to be useful. It needs only this. An adversary, whether by plan or by the patient accumulation of local advantages, learns to turn the strengths of an open system into the instruments of its own advance. Its deep markets, its generous disclosure, its rule-of-law habits — all of it becomes raw material for someone else's strategy. That is what the record shows. Naming it precisely is stronger than overclaiming a master plan the evidence cannot support.

## 5. The Insider Mint: How U.S. Capital Became Compensation

The least-examined channel is, for the purpose of understanding who benefited, the most important. The companies' own SEC filings let us quantify it directly.

A U.S. listing creates a liquid, dollar-denominated equity currency, the American Depositary Receipt, in which a company can pay its people. Chinese technology companies used that currency lavishly. The mechanism is simple and shows up in every annual report. The company grants new shares to its executives, engineers, and staff. The public float absorbs the dilution. The insider sells into the depth of the U.S. market and realizes dollars. The American investor's contribution is the standing bid that makes the equity worth granting.

The numbers come straight from the filings.[^14] Alibaba's fiscal-2016 annual report on Form 20-F discloses share-based compensation rising from RMB 1,254 million in fiscal 2012 to RMB 2,844 million in the 2014 IPO year and RMB 16,082 million by fiscal 2016, a roughly thirteen-fold escalation across the listing event. The U.S. IPO did more than fund the company. It repriced the company's entire internal equity currency.

The escalation ran on into the 2021 peak. The September 2020 quarter alone carried RMB 24,694 million of share-based compensation, and the full-year fiscal 2021 results attribute a single RMB 16,054 million year-over-year increase to Ant Group awards granted to Alibaba employees. Then came the reversal. The December 2024 quarter shows RMB 3,865 million against RMB 6,222 million a year earlier, the company explaining the drop as the result of fewer awards granted.

JD.com's filings trace the same arc. Compensation ran RMB 4,250 million in the 2014 IPO year, RMB 9,134 million at the 2021 peak, falling to RMB 2,999 million by 2024. The filings also expose the structure's hidden multiplier. JD Logistics, JD Health, JD Property, and JD Industrials each run their own share-incentive plans, consolidated into the U.S.-listed parent, so a single corporate group operates several equity mints at once.

The pattern holds down the size scale. Vipshop's share-based compensation rose from RMB 688 million in 2019 to RMB 1,538 million in 2024. Trip.com's ran from RMB 1.2 billion to RMB 2.0 billion across 2022 to 2024. Baidu's exceeded RMB 5 to 7 billion a year. Bilibili disclosed RMB 4.5 billion of compensation still queued to vest at the end of 2021.

Sum only the issuers and years cited here, from their own filings, and the total exceeds RMB 200 billion, roughly thirty billion dollars. These are a handful of 161 issuers. The cohort-wide total over the decade plausibly runs well past one hundred billion dollars of grant-date value, equity whose worth existed only because U.S. and global public-market investors stood ready to absorb the dilution.

The shape over time is the proof. Share-based compensation across these filings climbs into the 2020 and 2021 U.S. valuation peak and falls fifty to seventy percent by 2024, as ADR prices and American appetite collapsed. Equity pay is worth granting only when someone will buy the shares. The someone was the American public market. When that bid withdrew, the compensation machine throttled down.

This is direct evidence, in the companies' own audited filings, that U.S. capital served as the operating subsidy for the Chinese technology sector's competition for elite talent. It was talent the sector bid away from American firms, using a currency American investors underwrote.

A second subsidy ran on price. U.S. growth-market valuations between 2014 and 2021 were the most generous in the world, and listing in New York rather than Shanghai meant selling a smaller share of the company for more money. The spread between the multiple American buyers paid and the multiple a domestic listing would have fetched is a measurable transfer. It runs from the buyers, disproportionately U.S. institutions and index funds, to the sellers — the founders, the pre-IPO venture funds, and the companies' own treasuries.

## 6. Differentiated Responsibility: Who Actually Pays, and for What

The report's usefulness to law-enforcement readers depends on this section, and it rests on one discipline. The conduct in this record divides into three categories, and conflating them destroys the credibility of the whole. A prosecutor's first questions are which statute, which defendant, and what evidence. A document that answers "all of them, for everything" answers nothing.

### Category one: adjudicated wrongdoing, where the system worked

Some of the conduct here was criminal or sanctionable, and courts and regulators have already punished it. This chapter cites these cases not as open matters but as proof that the pattern is real and that the existing tools can bite.

Luckin Coffee paid a $180 million SEC penalty for a documented fabrication of more than $300 million in sales. The PCAOB, once it secured access, imposed $7.9 million in fines in November 2023, including $7 million against the PwC affiliates in China and Hong Kong for failing to stop more than a thousand of their personnel from cheating on internal-training examinations, and a separate sanction against the mainland firm Shandong Haoxin for falsifying audit reports.[^15] In March 2024 it sanctioned three partners of KPMG's China affiliate over their audit of a U.S.-listed Chinese issuer that had intentionally inflated its revenue. In November 2024 it revoked the registration of another Chinese firm for repeated rule violations.

Where American regulators could reach the conduct, they acted. The lesson is not that regulators did nothing. It is that they could act only after a decade in which no one could inspect the books at all.

### Category two: lawful conduct, however harmful

The underwriting of these offerings was the work of the world's largest investment banks, and it was lawful. The Commission's table names the syndicates issuer by issuer. Underwriting a disclosed, SEC-registered security is not a crime, and casting the banks that did it as criminal perpetrators would be both false and self-defeating, because the first such overstatement hands every reader a reason to throw out the whole report.

The same holds for the index providers whose inclusion decisions channeled passive money, and for the pension trustees who allocated to the China trade within their mandates. The right answer to lawful-but-harmful conduct is not prosecution. It is reform — the disclosure, fiduciary, and listing-standard changes set out in Section 8. And where intermediaries downplayed material risks to fiduciary clients, the ordinary remedies of fiduciary and disclosure law apply, on their own facts.

### Category three: open questions, warranting investigation that has not occurred

Between the punished and the plainly lawful lies the territory a referral to the Department of Justice and to state attorneys general can address, and the framing must be exact. **These are facts warranting investigation, never findings of guilt.**

They include several questions. Did specific issuers make material misstatements about the nature or enforceability of their VIE arrangements after regulators tightened the disclosure obligations in July 2021? Did anyone conceal undisclosed party-state control or direction from investors in particular cases? Did any audit firms keep obstructing inspection after the 2022 agreement? Did the marketing of these securities to U.S. retail and pension investors involve misrepresentations actionable under state securities, or blue sky, laws? And does the continued, unrestricted solicitation of American investment into companies formally designated as Chinese military companies raise issues under existing sanctions and disclosure authorities?

This chapter asserts none of these as established. Each is a question the public record makes reasonable, and each is the proper subject of the investigative tools that only government holds. Subpoena power, compelled testimony, access to non-public filings, which a private report does not possess.

The discipline of this section is the report's spine. It is also exactly what separates a document a serious prosecutor will read from one a consulting firm or an advocacy shop will produce. Not heat, but the precise allocation of conduct to category, and of remedy to conduct.

## 7. The Opportunity Cost and the Concentration of Gains

Two questions remain to close the economic account. What the diverted capital might have done instead, and who captured what.

The opportunity cost admits no exact figure, but its order of magnitude is stark. The capital that flowed into the VIE cohort over fifteen years is several multiples of the $52 billion the United States committed under the CHIPS Act to rebuild domestic semiconductor capacity.[^16] It is comparable to the entire venture funding of the American AI sector through 2023. It is large beside the cumulative equity issuance of the U.S. semiconductor industry over the period.

Every dollar of American pension money bidding for a Chinese ADR was a dollar not bidding for a U.S. small-cap industrial, a domestic chip-equity offering, or an American startup, at the very moment U.S. policy had begun spending public money to rebuild the industrial base that private capital had declined to fund.

A return-based measure also exists. The principal China equity benchmark delivered roughly zero net return to U.S. investors across the 2010 to 2024 period taken as a whole, against a several-fold gain in the broad U.S. market. On realized returns alone, before counting a single fraud or forced delisting, the diversion of American savings into the China trade cost American savers an enormous sum in foregone gains.

The distribution of what gains there were is the heart of the few-hands question, and the record supports a three-way split.

**Chinese founders and insiders captured the equity creation.** The fortunes, the share-based compensation quantified in Section 5, the valuation spread.

**The financial intermediaries captured the fees.** Underwriting spreads on tens of billions of dollars of primary issuance, typically four to seven percent, plus follow-ons, plus a continuing annuity of trading, custody, and index-licensing revenue. The same handful of banks appears across the great majority of the cohort's value.

**And the Chinese party-state captured the strategic asset.** A technology sector built to scale on foreign capital, over which it keeps legal and practical command.

The residual claimant, in every sense, was the diversified American saver, who bore the dilution, the frauds, the regulatory write-downs of 2021 and 2022, the zero-recourse delistings, and the flat aggregate return.

## 8. What Follows: Remedies Grounded in Existing Authority

A case study earns its place in this book only if it points to action, and the action here is a graduated ladder. Each rung rests on authority that already exists rather than on a wished-for new regime. The ladder runs from the least disruptive to the most, and a serious program would climb it in order, because each rung that works reduces the need for the next.

**Disclosure, perfected.** The Gensler reforms of July 2021 require VIE issuers to disclose the shell-versus-operating-company distinction, the enforceability risk, and the party-state-approval status. The first rung is to make that disclosure unmissable rather than merely present. It should be front-of-document, plain-language, at the point of sale to retail investors. Extend it to the index and fund products through which most exposure now flows, so the schoolteacher's target-date fund discloses what it actually holds.

**Index exclusion.** The single highest-leverage and least-disruptive move is to close the involuntary channel. Require or induce MSCI and FTSE Russell to exclude VIE-structured issuers from the benchmarks that U.S. retirement money tracks, or mandate such exclusion for ERISA-governed and public-pension fiduciaries, as the Thrift Savings Plan already did for itself. This stops the automatic allocation of American savings into the structure without forcing a disorderly sale that would itself harm current U.S. holders. This rung answers the protect-ordinary-people objective with the smallest collateral damage.

**Conditional delisting, through the statute that exists.** Forced delisting already has machinery. The Holding Foreign Companies Accountable Act delists issuers whose auditors the PCAOB cannot inspect. The 2022 agreement paused that clock. The rung here is to make PCAOB access permanent and verifiable, and to re-trigger the delisting timer automatically and at once upon any renewed obstruction. The 2023 raids on the due-diligence firms Mintz Group and Bain are the standing proof that obstruction is not hypothetical.[^17] This delists not by fiat but by the failure of the audit access the post-Enron settlement guarantees every other issuer.

**An ownership requirement, the cleanest structural fix.** The most direct remedy for a structure whose defining flaw is non-ownership is to require ownership. An issuer would have to hold actual equity in its operating business, not merely contractual claims, as a condition of listing. This rests on the same ownership-versus-contract distinction the SEC Chair placed on the record in 2021, and it treats the disease rather than the symptom. It is the highest rung because it is the most disruptive to existing listings, which is why it sits last, and regulators would reach it only if the lower rungs fall short.

**Enforcement, on its own facts.** Running alongside the ladder, the Category-three questions of Section 6 are the proper subject of referral to the Department of Justice and to state attorneys general, framed, as that section insists, as facts warranting the investigative tools only government holds, not as concluded guilt. The PCAOB enforcement program, the SEC's fraud authority, and the closing of the loophole by which the 1260H military-company designation carries no investment restriction before 2026 complete the enforcement layer.

**The patent channel has its own short ladder**, drawn straight from Choate's twenty-year-old recommendations that no one ever adopted. Restore the publication exemption, so inventors who do not seek foreign patents can keep their applications confidential until grant. Fund the examiner corps to collapse the pendency window during which inventions sit exposed. And condition foreign access to the U.S. patent database on reciprocal enforcement, denying the open library to jurisdictions that offer American inventors no protection in return.

## 9. The Honest Counterweights

A case study that cannot state what cuts against it is not evidence. It is a brief. Four counterweights belong in the record, and stating them strengthens the argument rather than weakening it, because the argument survives them.

**First, not all outcomes were losses.** Early holders of Alibaba, NetEase, Trip.com, and others earned large absolute returns over long periods, and diversification into the world's second-largest economy was a defensible mandate for most of the period in question. The structural critique is not that the structure harmed every investor. It is that the intermediaries collected fees regardless of outcome while the claim sold to investors was one Chinese courts had never shown they would enforce.

**Second, causation runs both ways.** Chinese technology would have found funding with or without American exchanges, through Hong Kong, the domestic STAR Market, or private capital, so the marginal contribution of U.S. listings is real but not total. What New York uniquely supplied was scale, valuation, and prestige, not the bare existence of the funding.

**Third, the legal characterization matters.** These were disclosed, SEC-registered offerings, and the VIE risks appear, in increasingly blunt language since 2021, in every prospectus. The system's failure was not concealment in the narrow criminal sense. It was a gatekeeping architecture in which every paid participant had incentives to process the deal and no one earned a fee for refusing it.

**Fourth, the trend has partly self-corrected.** U.S. holdings of Chinese securities roughly halved from the 2020 peak.[^18] The Thrift Savings Plan excluded China. A 2023 executive order restricted U.S. outbound investment into Chinese AI, semiconductors, and quantum computing. The post-2024 listing pipeline shrank to a thin tier of small-cap boutiques. The problem is not static, and an honest account says so.

None of these dissolves the core finding. They bound it. The finding that survives all four is narrower than destruction, and more durable for being precise. For a quarter-century the United States ran the world's deepest capital market and most generous patent system with no mechanism for pricing the difference between owning a company and holding an unenforceable contract on one, and no mechanism for weighing the strategic cost of capitalizing a rival's technology base.

That is not the collapse of American markets. It is something more troubling, because it was lawful, systematic, and is in large part still permitted. It was a sustained transfer of wealth and strategic advantage, carried out in plain sight, through the front door, with the willing participation of the institutions the system trusts most.

---

## 10. Summary: What the Case Study Proves

Return, finally, to the framework. The dimensions chapters held that modern contests are waged across the axes of capital, information, law, technology, and time. The domains chapters held that those dimensions resolve in identifiable arenas. This case asked whether the apparatus could explain a complete, documented episode using all of it at once.

It can. The borrowed-knife maneuver runs through every dimension. A one-way capital valve, an asymmetric information regime, a territorial enforcement gap, a convergence of the capital and patent channels on the same strategic technologies, and a quarter-century of legible evolution. It resolves in every domain. The listing, the audit, the fraud, the savings complex, the patent system, the defense-industrial base. It exhibits the stratagems — the borrowed knife and the crossing of the sea, the sacrifice of the plum to save the peach, the fire watched from across the river, the looting of the burning house — while staying honest about where the evidence shows design and where it shows only emergence. And it ends not in lament but in a graduated set of remedies built on authority that already exists, and in a disciplined, category-by-category allocation of responsibility a prosecutor can actually use.

The maneuver succeeded for as long as it did, not because the American system was weak, but because it was open, and openness premised on a reciprocity that never arrived is not a strength. It is an exposed flank.

Picture the far end of it. The capital lands in Beijing, the technology lands in Beijing, the command over both lands in Beijing, and the men who carried it across collect their fee and call it a good year. The Party did not need to fight for the knife. It watched us forge it, polish it, and lay it on the table, and it watched our own bankers hand it over with a smile.

The knife was borrowed because we left it out. The work of the chapters that follow, and of the appendices and the full investigative report this chapter introduces, is to show exactly how someone took it up, exactly who handled it, and exactly how to take it back, in the fullest documentary detail the public record allows.

### A note on quotation, and three corrections made in this edition

This chapter previously reproduced source material at lengths this book does not permit, and the passages have been rewritten.

The most serious instance was in §2.5. The draft quoted a Chinese consumer-electronics executive, via Choate's paper, for forty-four consecutive words describing the company's patent-mining practice. That is far beyond any reasonable quotation limit for a secondary source reproducing a third-party interview. The passage is now paraphrased, retaining a single short attributed phrase where the executive's own wording is genuinely load-bearing — the admission that the practice let the firm spend very little on research.

The second instance was in the Prologue. The draft quoted Chairman Gensler's July 2021 statement twice, at twenty-two and twenty-one words. Both are now paraphrased. The substance is unchanged: the regulator stated on the record that neither the shell nor its shareholders own the operating business, and that he doubted ordinary investors understood this.

The third was the Beijing analyst's characterization of the Party's 2020 opinion, shortened here.

**In each case the paraphrase carries the full meaning.** Where a source's exact wording does real work — a regulator's formal admission, a corporate denial later proven false — this chapter retains a short quotation and attributes it precisely. Luckin's four-word denial is kept verbatim because its falsity is the point.

All figures from the Commission's March 2025 report are exact to that source.
