Prologue

Two Men With Nothing

In October 1935, a column of exhausted men reached the hills of northern Shaanxi. They had walked for a year. Accounts of how many set out vary, and the Party that later told the story had reasons to shape it, but the range in the serious sources runs from eighty thousand to a hundred thousand at the start and roughly eight thousand at the end.[1] Nine in ten did not arrive. Party membership had fallen from something near three hundred thousand to perhaps forty thousand. They had abandoned their base, lost their supply lines, buried their wounded where they fell, and marched six thousand miles to a cave district so poor that the local peasants lived in hollows dug out of the loess.

Mao Zedong settled at Yan'an in December 1936 with a broken army, no industry, no navy, no air force, no foreign recognition, and no money.

Thirteen years later he had China.

His opponent had everything. Chiang Kai-shek's Nationalist government held the cities, the ports, the customs revenue, and the recognition of every major power. It had an army several times the size of anything Mao commanded. It had American aid, American aircraft, American advisers, and after 1941 the world's largest economy as a formal ally. On paper the contest was not close, and on paper it was never close, right up until the moment the Nationalists boarded boats for Taiwan.

Now move the clock forward and the map south.

The second man with nothing had spent thirty years abroad before he had a country to lead. Ho Chi Minh worked as a kitchen hand in London, a photograph retoucher in Paris, a Comintern agent in Moscow and Canton. He petitioned the powers at Versailles in 1919 for Vietnamese self-determination and was ignored. He founded the Indochinese Communist Party in 1930 from exile. He returned to Vietnam in 1941, after three decades away, and established a base in a cave in the northern hills, the same choice Mao had made at Yan'an, made independently, for the same reasons.[2]

Ho was the strategist. What he needed was an instrument.

In December 1944, in a forest in northern Vietnam, a former history teacher named Vo Nguyen Giap assembled a military unit on Ho's instruction. It consisted of thirty-one men and three women. They carried flintlock rifles. Giap had never attended a military academy and never would. He liked to say that the only academy he had known was the bush, and he claimed his understanding of grenades came from an encyclopedia entry.

Ten years later that unit had become an army that destroyed a French expeditionary force at Dien Bien Phu, hauling artillery over mountains by hand, piece by piece, in a fifty-five-day siege that ended French colonial rule in Indochina.[3] Twenty years after that, the same movement outlasted the United States of America, whose military at that moment was the most powerful instrument of destruction ever assembled by any nation in history. On the thirtieth of April 1975, its tanks came through the gates in Saigon.

Two movements. Two starting positions close to zero. Two of the largest military and economic powers on earth on the other side of the ledger. Both times, the side with almost nothing won, and won permanently.

The obvious explanations do not hold.

It was not ideology. Communist movements were crushed in Malaya, in Greece, in the Philippines, in Indonesia, in Bolivia. Believing the doctrine did not make anyone win.

It was not terrain, or patience, or the willingness to absorb casualties, though each of those mattered. Plenty of movements had rough country and time and a tolerance for their own dead, and were destroyed anyway.

It was not that the losing side was incompetent. Chiang's officers included capable men. American commanders in Vietnam won essentially every engagement they chose to fight, which is precisely the fact that ought to trouble a professional reader, because they won the battles and lost the country. In 1968 the Tet Offensive was, by every measure the American military was counting, a catastrophic defeat for Hanoi. It was also the moment the war turned. Two armies were on that battlefield fighting two entirely different wars, and only one of them knew it.

And it was not luck. Luck does not repeat across two countries, two enemies, and forty years.

What both movements had was a method. They fought in every domain at once, on a horizon long enough for the combination to compound, under a single command that measured all of it against one aim.

Military force was one instrument among many, and not the decisive one. Land reform was an instrument. Propaganda was an instrument. Recruiting the enemy's own population, the enemy's own press, and the enemy's own political opposition were instruments. So was the calendar. Both movements were prepared to lose for twenty years in order to win in the twenty-first, against opponents who measured success in electoral terms and budget cycles.

And the method ran on people who mostly did not know they were part of it. The land reform cadre who handed a peasant a title deed was recruiting an army. The Nationalist officer who took a bribe was doing the movement's work without being paid by it. The foreign reporter who filed an accurate story about a corrupt garrison was an instrument, and so was the lawmaker who voted to cut an appropriation for reasons entirely his own. None of them enlisted. The method does not require enlistment. It requires only that the incentives of enough separate actors happen to point the same direction, and it treats arranging that as a task of war.

Their enemies were formidable inside single domains and could not combine them.

That sentence is the whole book.

This book calls that method Total Domain War, and makes three claims about it.

It is not Chinese. The method is what winning has always required, and the clearest modern demonstration of it was run by the United States between 1941 and 1945, when America fused industry, science, finance, information, culture, and arms into a single instrument and out-produced the Axis powers combined in munitions.[4] The Second World War proves the thesis twice over, by two powers that barely understood each other. Americans are not strangers to this method. They are among its most successful practitioners, and they have forgotten it.

It is being run now, against the West, and it is working. The chapters that follow document the mechanism in twelve domains and dozens of specific, sourced operations, and they document why a materially superior alliance keeps arriving too late and in pieces.

And the reason it works is not something the adversary built. It is something the West built for itself, over roughly a century, one defensible decision at a time. That is the least comfortable claim in this book and the one everything else rests on.

A final matter, and nothing in this book works without it.

The method that defeated Chiang Kai-shek was turned on the Chinese people within a decade of the victory, and it killed them in numbers that dwarf anything the Nationalists managed. The famine that followed the Great Leap Forward is the deadliest in recorded history. The Cultural Revolution turned a civilization against its own teachers. In 1989 the same instrument was aimed at unarmed students. It is aimed today at Uyghurs in Xinjiang, at Hong Kong, and at every Chinese citizen who would like to say a true sentence out loud.

The Chinese Communist Party is not China. The Chinese people are this method's first and longest-suffering victims, not its authors, and no argument in this book requires or permits the reader to confuse the two. Any reader who finishes it with a grievance against Chinese people has read it exactly backward.

The subject here is a method, who understands it, and what it costs a free society not to.

Four Boxes

Set the two powers against the two eras and you get four boxes. The book is an argument about what is in them.

China then had it. Mao's movement ran every domain it could reach. Land reform was an instrument. Propaganda was an instrument. The peasantry was an instrument, and so was the enemy's own corruption, and so was time. It fought on a horizon measured in decades against an opponent measuring in campaigns.

China now has it. The Party never put the instrument down. It won in 1949 and kept the machinery, and it has spent the decades since running the same method against its own population and, more recently, outward. Twelve domains, one committee, one aim, and a stated horizon reaching to 2049. The chapters ahead document that in detail.

America then had it. This is the box most readers do not expect, and it carries the argument. Between 1941 and 1945 the United States fused industry, science, finance, information, culture, and arms into a single instrument and out-produced the Axis in the materials that decide wars. Before that, Grant and Sherman fought a war of blockade, railroads, industry, manufacturing, and proclamation, not merely of battles, and Lincoln issued the Emancipation Proclamation as a diplomatic weapon aimed at London as much as a moral one aimed at home. Pershing built an army of two million from almost nothing in nineteen months. The method is not foreign. Americans have run it, at the largest scale it has ever been run, and they won.

America now does not have it. Not for lack of resources, which remain the largest in the world. Not for lack of skill inside any single domain, where American institutions still run at or near the top. What is missing is the ability to combine them. Seventeen intelligence agencies produce assessments no single authority reconciles. One department funds what another forbids. The horizon has compressed to the electoral cycle and the budget year.

Three of the four boxes are full. One is empty, and it is the one the reader lives in.

That is the whole argument, and the rest of this book is the evidence for it.

What follows examines the men who ran the method and what it did for them, then the ten dimensions the method operates through, the twelve domains it operates in, the thirty-six stratagems it operates by, the documented cases where it has been run against the West, and finally the architecture that would be required to answer it.

The one question left over is the hardest. If Americans had this once, why can they not simply pick it up again?

The answer occupies the second half of this book, and it is not that the country grew weak or that its people changed. Nothing about the American character changed.

The architecture changed, and it changed by accumulation rather than by any decision anyone made.

Stated as inference, not finding

The central claim of this prologue, that two movements separated by geography, era, and enemy won by the same method rather than by circumstance, is the argument the rest of the book exists to support. The individual facts are documented. The common method is argued, and the reader is entitled to withhold assent until the evidence arrives.

The distinction this book holds from its first page

The Party is not China, and the Chinese people are the method's first victims rather than its authors. That distinction is stated here, restated at the head of Part Five, and maintained in every case chapter. It is not a courtesy. It is the analytical key, because a reader who conflates the regime with the population will misread every case that follows and will draw exactly the conclusion the Party benefits from.
