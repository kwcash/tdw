## The Roadmap

*From Argument to Sequence*

An architecture board can convene, hold a specification and publish it. A board that opens with seventy-five demands at once has published a wish list instead of a roadmap. TOGAF has a name for the discipline that turns a target architecture into something an institution can build. Migration planning asks a narrower question than "what should exist." It asks what should happen first, what should happen once that is in place, and what only becomes possible after both.

This book's case studies produced a target table in every chapter, one gap and one fix at a time. Read alone, each fix looks reasonable. Read together, seventy-five of them would overwhelm any legislature, any agency and any budget cycle presented all at once. Sequencing is not a compromise on the argument. The sequence itself marks the difference between an architecture and a demand.

### Three Transition Architectures

TOGAF sorts a migration into stages it calls transition architectures, each a working state on the way to the target rather than the target itself. This book sorts its own seventy-five elements into three.

**Now.** Elements that need no new statute, because existing authority already covers them. A hardware bill of materials before a port accepts new equipment, as *Protocols and Ports* argues. Disclosure rules already within an agency's existing power to write. Response packages agencies agree on in advance, under authority they already hold, rather than negotiating a crisis from a standing start. Address-confidentiality protection for a target under pressure from abroad, as *The Long Arm* argues. Labels on state-linked accounts that identify without removing lawful speech. None of these needs Congress. Most need only a decision to act.

**Next.** Elements that need one statute or one rule, not a program of them. The convening council itself, with a narrow charter and a long enough life to outlast an election. Harmonized designation lists, so that one finding at one agency triggers a defined review at the others instead of sitting unread. A statute naming coercion carried out on a foreign government's behalf as the crime, rather than treating a target's foreign ties as the suspicious fact. Emergency financial and public-health powers written with objective standards and sunsets before the next emergency arrives, not during it.

**Later.** Elements that require years and money before they exist at all. Allied crane and chip production, the kind *Protocols and Ports* and *Taiwan* both price at years and higher unit costs. A dependency map that shows which allied ports, suppliers and platforms already run on the systems this book's cases describe. A standards workforce funded well enough that a small firm or a university can afford to send an engineer to the room where the rules get written.

### What the Sequence Buys

The three stages are not a schedule for one office to work through in order. They are three different kinds of decision, made by three different kinds of authority, on three different clocks. A department can act on Now this quarter. Next needs a Congress willing to pass one statute rather than none. Later needs years regardless of who is willing, because capacity takes the time it takes to build.

Reading the roadmap this way answers the question *Coherence Without Command* leaves open. An architecture board with no power to command still has to choose where to spend its first year of attention, and the answer is Now, not Later. A board that opens with allied chip fabs and closes with a hardware bill of materials has its priorities backward. The building starts with the fixes an office can adopt on its own authority, tomorrow, because those are the only fixes that do not require anyone to wait for permission that may never come.

### Where the Full Roadmap Lives

Appendix F carries all seventy-five elements from every case in this book. Each carries its owner and its cost, drawn from that case's own target table. All seventy-five sort into Now, Next and Later in full. This chapter has named the shape. The appendix names every piece.
