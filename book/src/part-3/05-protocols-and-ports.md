## Protocols and Ports

*The Logical Layer and the Physical Layer of the Same Supply Chain*

**DOMAINS** Kinetic · **Economic (spine)** · Information · **Legal (spine)** · Association · Cyber · AI and Cognitive · Data and Surveillance

**DIMENSIONS** **Time (signature)** · Entropy · Topology · Asymmetry · Agency · Resonance · Phase · Gradient · **Criticality (signature)** · **Coherence (master)**

**STRATAGEMS** Spine S4 · S8 · S10 · S17 · S24 · S30 · S35

**GAPS** Six, one at each layer. Two signature gaps. Principles, where the defenders' own controls kept their engineers out of the room. Technology, where the cranes carried a channel their owners never asked for.

In November 2016 engineers from around the world met in Reno and settled the coding scheme for the control channel of 5G mobile networks. They chose polar codes, a scheme Huawei had championed. Every 5G phone and base station in the world would carry it. \[1\]

Cut from the meeting room to the waterfront. Nearly 80 percent of the ship-to-shore cranes at American ports come from one maker, Shanghai Zhenhua Heavy Industries. Known as ZPMC, the company is owned and operated in the People's Republic. \[2\] A standard fixes how a network talks. A crane is what moves the cargo the network describes. One is the logical layer of the supply chain and the other is the physical layer, and the two get read here as the same architecture question asked twice, at different altitudes.

The record documents both events. Reading them as one contest over the supply chain's architecture, logical and physical, is the framework's argument.

### The Terms of This Case

TOGAF gives every enterprise a Technology Architecture, the platforms and infrastructure its applications run on. A telecommunications standard and a shipping crane both belong to that layer, however different they look on the surface. A protocol fixes how devices talk, which functions sit in the network and which sit at the edge. A crane fixes what physically moves and what channels run inside the machine that moves it. Engineers write the world's standards in the International Telecommunication Union and 3GPP, in the Internet Engineering Task Force, and in ISO and the IEEE. A much smaller set of manufacturers build the machines that make those standards physical at a port, and one of those manufacturers builds most of them.

Participation in standards bodies is legitimate, and Chinese engineers contribute excellent technical work. A standard that wins on merit is good engineering, and this case does not treat any contribution as suspect because of its source. The same holds at the waterfront. ZPMC's cranes are well built and sell at a price few competitors can match, and the workers who build them are not agents of anyone. The case concerns the Party's strategy for the bodies and the enterprises, never the engineers who sit at a standards table or the welders who staff a crane factory.

The actor throughout is the Party. ZPMC, the shipping giant COSCO and China Merchants are state-owned enterprises, and the standards delegations answer to a national outline the Party's Central Committee co-issued. Neither fact makes the individual engineer or the individual dockworker an instrument of the state. Both facts mean the enterprise and the delegation answer, in the end, to one authority neither layer's defenders can see clearly from where they sit.

### 1. Architecture Vision

**Documented.** In October 2021 the Party's Central Committee and the State Council jointly issued an outline for national standardization development, setting targets for converting international standards into national ones and raising the country's role in international standards bodies. The outline grew from a research program known inside the Party as China Standards 2035. \[3\]

The joint House committee report of September 2024 described ties between the People's Liberation Army and three state enterprises, ZPMC, COSCO and China Merchants. It noted a ZPMC heavy-lift ship the PLA used in an exercise, evidence that the line between a commercial fleet and a military auxiliary runs thinner than a bill of lading suggests. \[2\]

**Argued.** The aim is to write the rules the world's products must obey and to build the machines that carry those rules into every port. A protocol written to fit one country's industry or surveillance model governs every device that follows it, wherever that device sells. A crane built by one country's state enterprise carries whatever channel that enterprise designs into it, wherever the crane lifts. Neither instrument needs to be used maliciously to be useful. Both only need to exist, in enough places, for long enough.

### 2. The Party's Baseline

#### The Logical Layer. Standards

The Legal domain carried this half, because a standard works as law for machines.

**Economic.** Standard-essential patents earn royalties from every device that implements a standard, and Huawei ranks among the leading declarers of patents essential to 5G. \[4\]

**Legal.** The Party's courts have reached beyond its borders on standards patents. In September 2020 the Wuhan Intermediate People's Court barred InterDigital from pursuing its case against Xiaomi in other countries, before InterDigital had appeared in the Wuhan court. In December 2023 a court in Chongqing set the first global royalty rate by a Chinese court, for Nokia's patents in a dispute with OPPO, setting a lower rate for the mainland than for the American market. The European Union requested WTO consultations over such anti-suit injunctions on February 18, 2022. \[5\]

**Association.** A Chinese national, Zhao Houlin, served as Secretary-General of the ITU from 2015 to 2022. \[6\]

**Cyber.** In September 2019 Huawei and state-owned partners proposed a redesign of internet protocols at the ITU, known as New IP. Critics said it would move control into the network and make it easier to shut off or trace users. The proposal did not advance. \[7\]

**AI and Cognitive.** In 2019 mainland firms proposed ITU standards for facial recognition and video surveillance, drawn from systems in use at home. \[7\]

**Stratagem 30, Make the Host and the Guest Exchange Places**, is the signature routine. A newcomer to a body contributes steadily, gains seats and chairs, and becomes the host that sets the agenda. **Stratagem 8** pairs open technical contributions with attached patent positions. Its full name is Openly Repair the Gallery Roads, March Secretly Through Chencang. **Stratagem 4, Wait at Ease for the Weary Enemy**, outlasts delegations that attend only when their firms can spare the travel.

#### The Physical Layer. Ports and Cranes

The Economic domain carried this half, because the capability rests on building the world's port equipment more cheaply than anyone else.

**Kinetic.** Ports carry military cargo in war. A crane that stops, or a schedule that leaks, affects deployment.

**Economic.** ZPMC dominates the world market for ship-to-shore cranes. The Trade Representative found on January 16, 2025 that the Party had targeted the maritime, logistics and shipbuilding sectors for dominance. \[2\] \[8\]

**Cyber.** In September 2024 two House committees released a joint report finding cellular modems installed on cranes in operation at certain American ports, not needed to run the cranes, creating an obscure way to collect information and bypass the ports' firewalls. Some ports had declined remote monitoring in their contracts and found the modems anyway. ZPMC had repeatedly requested remote access and denied installing the modems. The report said the installer's identity remains unclear. \[2\]

**Data and Surveillance.** LOGINK is the Party's national transportation and logistics data platform. It tracks shipments, routing and cargo values drawn from customs forms. The Party offers it free of charge. More than twenty ports have partnered with it, including ports in Japan, South Korea and Europe. \[9\]

**Stratagem 10, Hide a Knife Behind a Smile**, offers the cheapest cranes and a free data platform alike. **Stratagem 17, Cast a Brick to Attract Jade**, gives away the platform to gain the data it carries back. **Stratagem 24, Borrow the Road to Conquer Guo**, rides commercial trade into the ports a military will use.

**Stratagem 35, Chain the Stratagems**, links both layers into one engine. A contribution earns a patent, and a patent earns a rate the Party's own courts can set for the world. The same pattern runs one level down. A cheap crane earns a contract, a contract earns a channel, and a channel earns a view of the port. The record documents each link at each layer. The chain across both is the framework's reading.

#### Governance. How Both Layers Held Together

**Time.** A standard lasts a generation of devices. Influence won in one meeting cycle pays for fifteen years, and a crane bought today runs for as long.

**Criticality.** A port is a chokepoint, and a crane is the chokepoint inside it. A protocol is a chokepoint too, quieter, sitting inside every device that implements it rather than on one waterfront.

**Coherence.** The Party's national outline coordinates delegations, champions and courts at the logical layer. State enterprises build the cranes, run the ships and operate the data platform at the physical layer. The record documents the outline, the ownership and the court cases. One design across both layers is the framework's argument.

### 3. The Defenders' Baseline

No single enterprise owns either layer, and at the logical layer that is deliberate. American standards are led by the private sector, voluntary and built on consensus. At the physical layer it is not deliberate. Port authorities are local, terminal operators are private, and federal agencies each hold a slice.

#### Business Architecture. Who Owned What

| Capability | Defender owner | Instrument |
|---|---|---|
| Coordinating federal use of standards | National Institute of Standards and Technology | Federal policy favoring voluntary consensus standards |
| Writing the standards | Companies, universities and engineers | Their own time and travel budgets |
| Export controls on standards-relevant technology | Commerce Department | The Entity List and its standards rules |
| Port operations and procurement | Local port authorities and private terminal operators | Contracts and lowest bids |
| Cyber defense of port operational technology | Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency | Guidance and advisories |
| Trade remedies at either layer | Trade Representative | Section 301 findings and fees |
| Domestic crane manufacturing | No domestic maker | None |

The export-control routine collided with the standards routine at the logical layer. Commerce placed Huawei on the Entity List in May 2019. It then answered the standards-participation question in pieces. An advisory opinion came in 2019. A rule covering Huawei alone followed in June 2020, a broader rule in September 2022, and another in July 2024. Commerce itself said the uncertainty had undermined American participation and leadership in standards work. \[10\]

The coalition routine worked when the defenders used it. On September 29, 2022 the American candidate Doreen Bogdan-Martin won the ITU's top post with 139 votes against 25 for Russia's candidate, out of 172 cast. \[6\]

At the physical layer, the defenders learned about the crane modems through a congressional investigation and port officials' own inspections. Congress acted on LOGINK in the defense act for fiscal 2024, barring the Pentagon from using any seaport that relies on the platform and barring federal funding for such ports. Allied ports in Japan, South Korea and Europe continued to partner with it regardless. \[9\] \[11\]

**The shipping clock**

| Date | Owner | Routine | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| March 2024 | Five labor unions | Petition to the Trade Representative | Investigation opened |
| January 16, 2025 | Trade Representative | Finding that the Party targeted shipbuilding and logistics | Grounds for remedies |
| October 14, 2025 | Trade Representative | Fees on Chinese-built and Chinese-operated ships | The Party imposed its own fees on American-linked ships the same day |
| November 10, 2025 | Both governments | Fees suspended for one year in a trade agreement | Due to resume November 10, 2026 |

The Party's answer went further than a mirror. Its fees reached ships owned or operated by Americans whatever their flag, and it sanctioned American units of a South Korean shipbuilder helping revive American shipbuilding. \[8\] The same trade agreement suspended Commerce's affiliates rule and the Party's rare-earth export rule, as *Military-Civil Fusion* and *The Digital Twin* record. All three instruments reach the end of their suspensions within days of each other around November 10, 2026. The record found for this edition shows none of the three extended, reversed or replaced. \[12\]

The physical layer also carries an unresolved case worth watching past this book's printing. CK Hutchison agreed in March 2025 to sell most of its global port portfolio, including the Panama Canal terminals at Balboa and Cristóbal, to a consortium led by BlackRock and MSC for $22.8 billion. The Party objected and pressed for COSCO to take a controlling stake in the buying consortium rather than the minority position first discussed. Panama's Supreme Court then ruled CK Hutchison's own concession for Balboa and Cristóbal unconstitutional, voiding it outright and removing the Panama terminals from the deal entirely. MSC's terminal arm took over Cristóbal on an interim contract while Panama prepares a fresh, competitive concession. Negotiations over the remaining forty-odd ports continued through the run-up to this edition without a signed agreement, still contested over whether a Chinese state carrier joins the buyer's side at all. \[13\]

#### Technology Architecture. The Platforms the Defenders Used

At the logical layer, the open internet runs on protocols from the Internet Engineering Task Force, where individual engineers participate on the merit of their work. That model helped keep New IP from advancing. \[7\] At the physical layer, the defenders' ports run on the cranes they could buy. With no domestic maker and costlier alternatives from Europe and Japan, ports bought on price. \[2\]

#### Principles and Governance. What Held the Defenders Together

The defenders keep their principles at both layers. Standards win on merit, companies bear their own costs, and no ministry dictates a vote. Ports run locally, buy competitively and trade openly. Those principles build good standards and bring cheap cranes. They also leave national interest in the standards bodies to whoever can afford to attend, and leave the question of what else a crane might carry to nobody in particular.

### 4. A Target Audience Note

The procurement officer who buys the crane, and the small firm that cannot afford to send an engineer to Geneva, fit this form together. Both are ordinary professionals making a defensible decision inside incentives nobody designed against them.

**Documented.** American participation in standards bodies rests on company and university travel budgets, and the 2023 National Standards Strategy called for a larger standards workforce without funding individual attendance. \[14\] Port procurement runs on competitive bids that score price and lifting capacity. No line item in that process asks what communication channels a crane carries. \[2\]

**Argued.** A target audience analysis would read this audience as the professional whose job rewards exactly the choice that leaves a gap open, not as anyone acting in bad faith. The standards engineer who skips a costly trip and the port official who accepts the cheapest qualified bid are both doing their jobs correctly by every metric either of them is measured against. Vulnerability runs through the metric, not the person. Susceptibility runs highest wherever a budget line has no security question attached to it. Accessibility requires no recruitment at all, because the audience does not have to be reached. It only has to be left alone.

### 5. The Formal Version of the Acceptance Test

NIST SP 800-207's zero trust rule, named in the Stack, verifies every request rather than trusting one permanently. Executive Order 14028 of May 2021 gave that rule a physical form for exactly this problem. It requires federal software suppliers to produce a software bill of materials, a list of every component inside a piece of code, so a buyer can check what it is acquiring rather than trusting the label on the box. \[15\]

A crane is not software, but it runs on software, firmware and a set of physical communication channels no procurement contract currently itemizes. A hardware bill of materials lists every chip, modem and channel in a piece of operational technology. That list is the crane acceptance test's formal name. The 2024 congressional report found modems that no contract had ordered because no bill of materials existed to check the crane against. The fix this chapter's target table proposes for Gap 5 is a hardware bill of materials by another name, applied to a machine instead of a codebase.

### 6. Gap Analysis

**The principles audit**

| Principle | The Party's design | The defenders' design |
|---|---|---|
| **Time** | Plans standards influence and crane dominance across decades | Attends where the next product needs it; found modems after years in service |
| **Entropy** | One outline aligns ministries, champions, courts and state enterprises | Firms, agencies, ports and diplomats each act separately |
| **Topology** | A national program behind delegations and a hub of state enterprises | Many firms and many ports, each for itself |
| **Asymmetry** | Reads every body and every port; offers free platforms abroad | Its own controls kept engineers out of rooms; barred a platform at home allies still use |
| **Agency** | Seats gained by attendance; access requested and, denied or not, obtained | Seats held only where firms pay; declined a service and got the channel anyway |
| **Resonance** | Contribution, patent, court, machine and data point one way | Standards, export, trade and procurement routines collide with each other |
| **Phase** | Contributes every cycle; answered fees the same day they were imposed | Took five years to settle one export question; suspended its own fees within four weeks |
| **Gradient** | State support tilts firms and buyers toward its standards and its cranes | Cost tilts small firms out of standards work and every port toward the cheapest bid |
| **Criticality** | Aims at protocols that fix control points and the chokepoint inside every port | Defended the open internet where engineers showed up; has no domestic crane maker |
| **Coherence** | Governed by a Party-issued outline and by state enterprises alike | A strategy with no owner of attendance, and a maritime stack with no owner at all |

The cells summarize documented acts. The audit itself is the framework's reading.

#### Gap 1. No Owner, at Either Layer

*Architecture Governance*

**Documented.** The 2023 National Standards Strategy set priorities without funding attendance. \[14\] Local authorities buy the cranes. The Coast Guard, CISA, Defense and the Trade Representative each hold a piece of the physical layer. None of the seven capabilities in the business-architecture table above answers to any of the others. \[2\] \[11\]

**Argued.** A private-sector model is the right model for standards, and it needs a public backstop it does not have. A locally run port system is the right model too, and it needs the same thing. Both gaps are the identical shape at two different altitudes, a good design principle left to fund and defend itself.

#### Gap 2. Years to Decide, Traded Away Within Weeks

*Architecture Principles, Time. Signature gap*

**Documented.** Commerce listed Huawei in 2019 and did not settle standards-participation rules until 2024. \[10\] The October 2025 port fees went into suspension by November 10, alongside two export-control rules. All three reach the same expiry date around November 10, 2026. \[8\] \[12\]

**Argued.** A control aimed at the adversary reached the defenders' own engineers for five years, and a standard fixed in those years governs a generation of devices, so the delay costs far longer than five years. A security instrument a trade negotiator can suspend in a matter of weeks becomes a bargaining chip rather than a defense, and three such chips now share one expiry date the defenders should meet with a decision already made rather than a negotiation still open.

#### Gap 3. Everything Public, Nothing Mapped

*Data Architecture*

**Documented.** Standards bodies publish officers, contributions and patent declarations. \[4\] LOGINK is free, partners with more than twenty ports and tracks cargo, value and routing. American law bars the Pentagon from ports that rely on it, and allied ports use it anyway. \[9\] \[11\]

**Argued.** Everything needed to see who holds which chairs, which patents and which data flows sits in public records at both layers. The defenders keep no national map of either one, so they notice a lost seat or an exposed port only after the fact.

#### Gap 4. The Capability Sits in the Adversary's Factories and Firms

*Business Architecture*

**Documented.** American standards participation rests on private budgets with no dedicated funding, and the 2023 strategy named the gap without closing it. \[14\] American ports depend on ZPMC for nearly 80 percent of their cranes, with no domestic maker and only costlier European and Japanese alternatives to turn to instead. \[2\]

**Argued.** Fields where American firms do not lead go unrepresented at the standards table, one missed meeting at a time, and the crane market shows what that same absence costs once it compounds for decades rather than years. Diversifying either capability costs money up front, against a competitor who built the current position over a horizon measured in plans, not quarters. The cost is the price of the chokepoint at whichever layer it sits, and the price only rises the longer the gap stays open.

#### Gap 5. No Test Before Acceptance

*Application Architecture*

**Documented.** The Party's courts set global patent rates through anti-suit injunctions the WTO case now contests. \[5\] Port officials found modems during inspections, including on cranes whose contracts had declined remote services, because no bill of materials existed to check against. \[2\]

**Argued.** Neither routine asks the acceptance question before the fact. A patent enters a standard without a public rate-setting check, and a crane enters a port without a communication-channel inventory. Both gaps close with the same instrument, an acceptance test performed before the thing is accepted rather than a dispute litigated after.

#### Gap 6. The Quiet Channel

*Technology Architecture. Signature gap*

**Documented.** The facial-recognition standards New IP's failure did not stop entered ITU study groups where fewer defenders attended. \[7\] The crane modems connected to control computers, served no operational need and could bypass firewalls the port thought it controlled. \[2\]

**Argued.** The open internet's defense held where engineers crowded the room, and it thinned in the quieter study groups where they did not. The port's defense held for lifting capacity and price, and it never extended to the channel riding along with both. An unrequested channel is the risk operational-technology security teams fear most, whether it rides in a protocol or in a machine, because it sits outside the boundary the owner believes it is defending.

### 7. Target Architecture

The target keeps standards private, voluntary and won on merit. It keeps ports open, local and competitive. At both layers it makes sure the defenders show up and know what they accepted.

**Gap to target**

| Gap | Target element | Owner | What it costs |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. No owner, at either layer | A funded owner that tracks standards attendance and convenes the maritime stack, without directing votes or nationalizing ports | NIST and the National Strategic Competition Council | Budget, and care to avoid a state line in private and local bodies |
| 2. Years to decide, traded in weeks | A standing rule that export controls never restrict published standards work, and security instruments kept out of trade bargains | Commerce, the White House and the Trade Representative | Scrutiny against loopholes, and less room to bargain |
| 3. Everything public, nothing mapped | A public map of standards seats and patents, and an open allied logistics data standard as an alternative to LOGINK | NIST, Transportation and allied governments | Analytic effort, and competition with a free product |
| 4. Capability sits abroad | Travel grants for standards participation, and allied or domestic crane production | Research agencies, Transportation and Defense, with allies | Money, and years to build capacity |
| 5. No test before acceptance | Allied cooperation against anti-suit injunctions, and a hardware bill of materials required before any operational-technology equipment is accepted | The Trade Representative and Port authorities, under Coast Guard rules | Diplomatic effort, and longer procurement |
| 6. The quiet channel | Expert attendance at surveillance-standard study groups, and removal or isolation of unrequested channels on operational technology | NIST, State and CISA | Time from scarce experts, and specialist staff |

**The coalition already won once.** The 2022 ITU election showed allies can hold a body's leadership when they organize. The target applies that same effort to the working groups where the architecture gets written, and to the ports where it gets built.

**What the target would not do.** It would not turn American engineers into ministry delegates or exclude a good contribution by its origin, and it would not close ports to the world's ships or treat a crane worker as an agent. Instead it would put the defenders in the room, and give every machine they accept an inventory. That claim remains an argument.

### What Protocols and Ports Proves

It proves Technology Architecture is one layer with two altitudes. A protocol and a crane look like different subjects until the same six gaps turn up in both, in the same order, for the same underlying reason.

It proves Time and Criticality together. A standard fixed once governs a generation of devices, and a chokepoint built once governs a generation of cargo. The side that shows up every cycle writes more of the future at both altitudes, and the side that shows up late inherits a design it did not choose.

It proves again that the defenders' own controls can do the adversary's work. An Entity List aimed at Huawei left American engineers unsure of their place in the room for five years, the same years a standard was being fixed for the world. Nobody in Washington intended that outcome. The architecture produced it anyway, the way a bureaucracy produces outcomes nobody in the room chose.

It proves that a chokepoint does not announce itself as one. A crane looks like a crane. A coding scheme looks like an engineering choice. Both are, right up until the day either one carries a channel or a rate or a court order that the buyer never priced into the decision to accept it.

### Field Card

**What happened.** Engineers meeting in Reno in 2016 fixed a 5G coding standard the whole world now carries, while export controls aimed at one company left American engineers unsure for five years whether they could even sit in the room where such decisions get made. At the same time, one state-owned company came to build most of the ship-to-shore cranes at American ports, some of which turned out to carry communication modems nobody had ordered. Standards and cranes are the same architecture layer, decided in a meeting room or built in a factory, and both arrived at American ports with no inventory of what exactly had been accepted.

**The gap.** No one owns attendance at the standards table, and no one owns the maritime stack, the same governance gap appearing at two different altitudes.

**The fix.** A funded standards-attendance program paired with a hardware bill of materials required before any port accepts new equipment would close both gaps with one design. Know who is in the room, and know what is in the machine.

**Where you could work on it.** Standards diplomacy at NIST, the State Department or an industry consortium. Supply-chain security for critical infrastructure. Export-control and trade-remedy law working the seam between security policy and negotiated trade deals.
